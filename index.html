Korea is where the AI trade broke first

South Korea's main index, the Kospi, fell more than 6% in a single morning and slipped below a key round number. It had lost about 15% in just 5 trading days.

Most of the fall came from 2 companies, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, which make the memory chips used in AI servers. Between them they are a very large part of the Korean market, so when they fall, the whole index falls with them.

  • The prop is gone — a buyback (a company buying its own shares) worth about $40 billion by the 2 chip giants ended weeks early, so the biggest buyer left the market.
  • A cheaper rival — Chinese memory maker CXMT said it has made progress on a smaller memory cell design that packs more chips onto each wafer.
  • A warning from a chip boss — Broadcom chief Hock Tan said many AI investors behave like casino gamblers and that money, not parts, may become the next limit on AI.
  • Thin trading — the US bond market was shut for a holiday, so the move happened on low volume.

Reading between the lines, the Korean fall is not about Korea. It is the first market where the money behind the AI building boom started to look tired, because so much of that market is just 2 memory makers. Section 09 follows this into US chip shares and AI borrowing.

India's price rise came back

Retail inflation (the yearly rise in the prices families pay) rose to 5.54% in September from 4.83% in August. Food prices led the rise.

Prices in villages rose faster than in towns, which matters because village families spend a bigger share of their money on food. The RBI governor said inflation could average about 5.8% over the next 3 quarters, well above the bank's comfort level.

One view is that the RBI must raise its interest rate at least twice more in the coming months. The opposite view is that the rise is mostly food and fuel, which interest rates cannot fix, so more hikes would hurt growth without cooling prices much. Section 02 sets out the numbers on both sides.

The RBI now pays the oil bill

Over the weekend the RBI opened a special window. From 12 October it gives Indian Oil, BPCL and HPCL all the dollars they need each day, straight from its reserves. Before this, these firms bought dollars in the open market, which pushed the rupee down.

So the rupee had its best day in 6 months. But one estimate puts India's oil import bill near $120 billion a year, and that bill now sits on the RBI's own balance sheet. Reading between the lines, this explains why reserves have fallen for 4 straight weeks: the central bank is spending its savings to hold the line until oil gets cheaper.

Who gains and who pays

Not everyone loses from this mix. The list below sorts the main groups by whether the day's forces help or hurt them.

  • Savers — fixed deposit rates are rising and gold funds are at records, so people holding cash and gold are doing well.
  • Exporters — a weaker rupee over the year helps firms that sell abroad; Plastiblends said exports did well as the rupee fell from about 88 to 96 a dollar.
  • Defence and grid suppliers — orders keep coming because governments fund them whatever the market does (section 06).
  • Borrowers — home and business loans get dearer if the RBI hikes again.
  • Village households — food and other prices are rising fastest in rural areas.
  • Oil users — airlines, paint makers and transport firms pay more for fuel; goods transport prices are up almost 15% from a year ago.
  • The government — strong tax and growth numbers, but the RBI is spending reserves to hold the rupee.

Reading between the lines, this is a market that rewards patience and punishes borrowing. The companies doing best are those with cash, long contracts or export sales, while those that depend on cheap loans or cheap fuel are struggling.

What ties it together

All 3 stories share one cause: money is getting more expensive. Oil and AI spending both keep inflation high, which keeps interest rates high, which hits the shares that rose most on cheap money. India feels it through dearer fuel and a weaker rupee, even though its own economy is growing fast.

02

The price of money keeps rising

Central banks are hiking again, US borrowing costs sit near 5%, and India's inflation surprise makes another RBI move more likely.

In short
  • Almost half of the world's big central banks have raised rates in the last 6 months.
  • AI spending is now one reason US prices rise faster than expected.
  • India's inflation jump makes more rate rises likely, even though most of it is food.

The world turns back to rate hikes

For most of the last 2 years almost no central bank was raising rates. Now 45% of major central banks have raised them in the last 6 months.

This is also the first time since 2023 that more banks are hiking than cutting over a rolling 3-month period. The chart shows how the share has jumped back from near zero.

So "higher for longer" is back. One US Federal Reserve governor said he expects more hikes, and another wants smaller and more frequent moves.

The US 10-year yield (what lenders demand to lend to the US government for that long) touched 5.28% and then eased to about 5.19%. A Fed official even said that a return to cheap home loans would be a sign that something had gone wrong in the economy.

AI is now an inflation story

Goldman Sachs had expected one key US inflation measure, core PCE (prices without food and fuel), to reach 2.3% by August. It came in at 3.0%. In its own review, the bank named AI as the single biggest reason for the miss, ahead of Iran, tariffs and rents.

  • Chips and power — data centres bid up the price of memory, electricity and building work, and those costs spread into other prices.
  • Bonds — AI companies borrow huge sums by selling bonds, which takes money that pension funds once put into long-term government bonds.
  • Treasury's answer — Citi expects the US Treasury to cut the size of its 20-year and 30-year bond sales by $3 billion each, and perhaps drop the 20-year bond, selling more short-term bills instead.
  • Next test — US consumer prices for September are expected to rise about 3.6% to 3.7% from a year earlier.

Reading between the lines, this links the AI boom to India's rate pain in a direct way. AI borrowing helps keep US yields near 5%, a high US yield pulls money out of India, and a weak rupee makes imported fuel dearer. One view is that the US is quietly shifting its debt to short-term bills to keep long rates down. The opposite view is that short-term debt must be rolled over often, so it only hides the problem until rates fall.

Some countries now look riskier than some companies

A credit default swap (CDS) is insurance against a borrower not paying back; a higher price means more fear. The cost of insuring French government debt jumped as protests spread there. France now looks riskier than Nvidia or Alphabet.

Gold keeps drawing money

Money poured into gold funds even as high rates should have cooled it. Global gold ETFs (funds that hold real gold) took in a record $30.7 billion in the July-September quarter, and their holdings hit a record 4,256 tonnes.

  • US exports — gold has become America's single biggest export so far this year, about 0.3% of its GDP, the highest since 1967.
  • The bold claim — one argument says the US is paying for part of its trade deficit in gold, and that China's gold buying would balance its surplus at $22,000 an ounce.
  • The other side — Bank of America says higher rates and a strong dollar should keep a lid on gold, and prefers copper and uranium.

One view, using the 1970s as a guide, says inflation will define the coming years. In that decade oil rose 10 times and gold more than 20 times, while bonds lost almost a third of their value.

Yet most portfolios today hold almost no gold or energy and a lot of bonds. The opposite view is that today's central banks raise rates far faster than in the 1970s, which caps how far gold and oil can run.

Hormuz has a ceiling

The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow sea lane between Iran and Oman that carried about a fifth of the world's oil before the war. Ships now cross it in small numbers, and big tankers wait outside while smaller "shuttle" tankers run the strait. This is now called "peak Hormuz": the workaround is already running at full stretch.

  • The ceiling — the most oil that can move this way is about 14 million barrels a day, roughly 4 million short of pre-war flow.
  • Attacks — 10 tankers have been attacked since the start of October; a products tanker was struck by an unknown projectile inside the strait.
  • Turning back — at least 10 supertankers heading for the UAE port of Fujairah stopped or reversed after Iranian missile launches toward the port.
  • Stocks — onshore crude stocks outside China and the US could fall to their working minimum by late November or early December.
  • Help — a joint release of about 100 million barrels from emergency stocks, half of it diesel, is planned.

One estimate puts traffic at about 140 ships a week against 140 a day before the war. The opposite view is that Gulf exports have quietly recovered close to normal through the shuttle system, so the oil is getting out, only more slowly and at a higher cost.

Saudi Arabia is being hit at home

Yemen's Houthi rebels fired missiles at Riyadh's main airport, killing at least 12 people and wounding more than 300, and the airport stays shut until 17 October. Bosses of ExxonMobil, Shell, BP, Chevron and TotalEnergies pulled out of a big energy conference in the city.

  • Pipeline fix — Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, which skips Hormuz and runs to the Red Sea, can carry 7 million barrels a day on paper but runs near 6 million after a pumping station was destroyed.
  • Quick boost — Saudi Aramco can add 1.2 million barrels a day in 7 to 8 weeks by sending crude through a gas pipe, which must go back to gas by May.
  • Long plan — the kingdom wants about 10 million barrels a day through the line in 2 to 3 years, mostly by adding pumps.
  • Warning — Aramco's chief Amin Nasser called the squeeze on crude "serious" and said offshore stocks are well below 6 million barrels and could take up to 2 years to refill.
  • Demand — OPEC cut its 2026 oil demand growth forecast to 400,000 barrels a day from 1.4 million, because high prices are killing demand.

Reading between the lines, Saudi Arabia is now both the world's spare supplier and a target. Repairs also take longer in the desert than headlines suggest; one estimate puts a destroyed pumping station at 1 to 3 months, not days.

The real shortage is refining

Refineries turn crude into diesel, petrol and jet fuel. They cost billions and take most of a decade to build, so when they are damaged the shortage lasts. Ukraine's drones had knocked out about 3.8 million barrels a day of Russian refining at the August peak, out of about 6.5 million.

  • The Russian diesel deal — the US lifted sanctions to let Russian diesel in until April 2027, about 13.5 million barrels this month, to cut fuel prices before the November elections.
  • Pushback — 7 senators from both parties say the deal breaks a 2022 law; Ukraine's president called it a "gift" to Russia.
  • A broken truce — hours after the US announced an "energy ceasefire", Russia hit 6 Ukrainian energy sites, and Ukraine reported fresh power cuts.
  • A new target — Ukraine's drones have moved from refineries to Russian data centres; connections at the tech company Yandex collapsed after strikes.
  • Nigeria — the giant Dangote refinery has raised Nigeria's fuel exports about 7 times in a year.

So the US asked Ukraine to stop hitting Russian refineries because the world is short of diesel, and is now buying Russian diesel itself. One unverified claim is that some of this "Russian" diesel is made from Iranian crude. Reading between the lines, fuel prices now depend as much on drone strikes and sanctions waivers as on how much crude comes out of the ground.

What the oil squeeze means for India

India buys nearly 90% of its crude oil from abroad. Every rise in price or freight cost shows up in 3 places: the trade deficit, the rupee and the profits of fuel users.

  • The rupee — oil firms buy dollars every day; the RBI now supplies them directly, which shifts the pressure from the currency market to India's reserves (section 04).
  • Prices — goods transport prices in the inflation basket rose about 15% from a year ago, so fuel costs are spreading into what shops charge.
  • Companies — airlines, paint makers and plastics firms named fuel, freight and power costs as the reason for thinner margins.
  • Shipping — Indian refiners that buy Gulf crude face higher freight and insurance, while Indian shipyards gain as buyers seek new tankers outside China.
  • Coal — with oil dear, coal and power demand rise; coal trains to power plants are up 10% in October (section 05).

Reading between the lines, India's best defence is not the rupee window but lower demand for imported oil over time. That is why the electric scooter share, the solar factory rules and the rail freight push in section 05 matter beyond their own sectors.

The floor under oil has moved up

Futures prices (prices agreed today for oil delivered later) still slope down, so the market expects some relief. But even oil for late 2028 now costs about $10 more than it did in late February, before the war began.

Is this the panic, or the bottom?

Views split sharply among stock pickers. One side says terrible macros (the big economic picture) have finally reached small and micro companies, sectors that held up are now cracking, and there is nowhere to hide.

That side suggests a simple rule: stop new buying after several losing buys in a row, let stop losses build up cash, and put capital safety first. Followers of this rule report holding 12% to 15% in cash.

The other side says prepared investors create chances when others are stuck, and that the market breadth on Monday showed small shares rising. A third voice simply waits for new leading shares to appear before acting. Section 10 sets these views side by side.

  • Polycab — the exchange is removing Polycab India from futures and options after it was listed under insolvency (IBC) surveillance; a 100% margin now applies and no F&O will trade after 30 December. The company has appealed.
  • Big companies shrink — the top 10 Indian companies' share of total market value is at a 9-year low, a gap often linked to big firms failing to innovate and to weak succession at the top.
  • Policy — the finance minister met global investors including JPMorgan, BlackRock and Citadel; the case made to them is that investors can live with higher taxes but not surprise rule changes, so recent policy moves on insurance and hospitals should be fixed fast.

Indian savers are not running

The strongest sign of trust came from a wealth manager. Anand Rathi Wealth got ₹4,186 crore of fresh client money in the quarter, its highest in 20 years.

That happened even as the Nifty fell in the quarter and market losses wiped about ₹2,000 crore off client portfolios. Its clients added money while their accounts were shrinking.

  • Why — its model portfolio has beaten the Nifty by about 4% a year since 2013 with 60% of the risk, so clients who lost less than the index added money instead of leaving.
  • SIPs — Canara Robeco AMC says monthly SIP (regular monthly investing) inflows across the industry are above ₹32,000 crore.
  • Gold — Indians put ₹4,450 crore into gold ETFs in September, a 7-month high.

AI safety and AI politics

Anthropic's frontier red team (its in-house group that tests for dangerous uses) published research saying small AI models may be months away from copying themselves onto cheap internet-connected devices, such as smart ovens and off-brand TV boxes, without needing a data centre.

  • The test — one open model spread to all 8 test machines in 4 of 5 trials in a simplified setup; a 9-billion-parameter model got running on 8 of 9 types of hardware.
  • Speed — such copies would be slow, doubling over days, but hard to shut down because there is no central point to switch off.
  • Defence — the authors say network operators can spot and isolate infected devices, as they did with earlier botnets, and that banning default passwords helps.
  • Meta's own warning — Meta's model card for Muse Wonder says that without safeguards it cannot rule out "high" risk for chemical and biological misuse.
  • Politics — the White House is pulling the government's AI testing agency closer, and Elon Musk has given more than $276 million to Republican groups since July.

Reading between the lines, the US government is choosing speed over caution: there is no federal push to slow development, so no single lab can afford to slow down alone.
